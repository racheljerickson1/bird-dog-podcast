About This Episode

In this episode I talk about training your dog to be nice and steady. No ones loves a dog that breaks before it's sent, flares birds and is a danger to its self jumping in front of guns or knocking over the blind being out of control. In this episode I share tips on how to steady your dog when self- training or with the help of another person. A steady dog is a beautiful thing and a joy to be around!

Transcript

Full transcript for Episode 40: Getting Your Dog Rock Solid Steady. The key to safety and killing more birds..

Tyce

Hey everyone, welcome to the Bird Dog Podcast. My name is Tyce. Today I want to talk about steadiness — what it actually means, why it matters for safety and for killing more birds, and the most effective way to build it into your dog.

Steadiness means the dog sits and stays until you send it. Not until the guns go off. Not until the bird falls. Until you say go. This sounds simple, but it has real consequences when it's missing. A dog that breaks early can flare birds that are still coming in — it bursts out of the blind right when the flock is rounding into range, and they're gone. More seriously, a dog that breaks in a layout blind or out of a boat jumps right past the muzzle of a loaded gun. That's how dogs get shot. Steadiness is a safety issue first and a hunting quality issue second.

When it works, it's one of the most satisfying things in the field. I was just on a hunt with five dogs. Birds came in, we shot, and we took turns releasing dogs one at a time — each one sent on its name, going out, making the retrieve, coming back, delivering to hand. No chaos, no tangled dogs, no one grabbing birds from someone else's dog. Five dogs, five guys, everything clean. That's what steadiness buys you.

Sending on your dog's name is important, and here's why: if you're hunting with multiple dogs and everyone releases on a generic command like "back" or "get it," every dog might go at once. But if each dog is trained to only go on its name, you control who goes when. If your dog is named Maverick, the command is just "Maverick." Say it, dog goes. Every other name is a test. I'll talk about testing in a minute.

For building steadiness, I like to use a place board — a small raised platform, roughly two by two feet, made from two-by-sixes. Nothing fancy. The key is that dogs are place-oriented: when they're on a raised surface, it's more obvious when they leave it. A dog sitting on the ground can creep forward subtly and it's easy to miss. A dog stepping off a place board is a clear, visible break. It raises the standard and makes it easier to enforce. If you've done early treat training, teach "place" as a positive command first — dog gets on, dog gets rewarded — then start building steadiness from there.

The sequence I work through: dog on place or at heel, give the mark cue, throw the bumper, wait. If the dog moves, no retrieve — walk out and pick it up yourself. Denial is the correction. Reset and go again. When the dog holds, send it on its name, let it make the retrieve. You're building the pattern that only a steady, quiet dog gets the retrieve. Everything else gets nothing.

I recommend doing force fetch before formal steadiness training. When you have command of the dog's mouth and it's retrieving cleanly, you can pattern a beautiful retrieve into every steadiness drill. You're not just working on sitting — you're working on the full sequence from mark to delivery to hand. The two programs reinforce each other.

One thing dogs will do once they're steady in training: they test you in new environments. Take them to a new field, a different pond, out of the normal routine, and they act like school's out. Stand your ground. Hold your standard. Dogs will try something once in a new place and then they'll settle down if you're consistent. Don't let a day of testing convince you they've forgotten everything — they haven't. They're just checking.

Finally, test your dog with realistic scenarios in training. While the dog is sitting steady, talk like you're on a hunt — great shot, nice bird, what was that — and throw other names out there. Joey. Sam. Hank. And then, after a pause: Maverick. The dog should stay locked in until it hears its name, no matter what else is being said. If you only ever practice in silence, the dog isn't prepared for what a real hunt sounds like. Build that into your drills and you'll have a dog that holds under real pressure. Thanks for listening.
