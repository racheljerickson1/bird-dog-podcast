Transcript

Full transcript for Episode 41: Whining and Barking Waterfowl Dogs & How Help Them Be Quiet..

Tyce

Hey everyone, welcome to the Bird Dog Podcast. My name is Tyce. I got a question from a client about something I see pretty often, especially in high-drive dogs: vocalization in the blind — whining, barking, breaking down when they're supposed to be sitting quietly. Today I want to talk about where this comes from and, more importantly, how to fix it.

First, context: dogs that vocalize in the blind almost always have a lot of drive. They're excited, they want the bird, and that energy has to go somewhere. A low-drive dog will often just sit there indifferent. The high-drive dog builds up so much anticipation that it starts to leak out — whine, bark, squirm. In that sense it's a good-problem version of a real problem. But it's still a problem. In the field it blows your cover. In a hunt test it'll cost you the pass. And it's just unpleasant to be around when the hunting's slow and the dog's going nuts in the blind.

The single most important thing to understand: if you send the dog on a retrieve while it's still whining, you've rewarded the whining. The retrieve is what the dog wants, and getting it while making noise tells the dog that making noise works. You may as well put the behavior on a training schedule. So the rule is simple — only send the dog when it's quiet. Not mostly quiet. Quiet.

Here's how to drill it. Keep it simple: dog at your side, mark, throw a bumper. If the dog whines or moves, don't send it. Walk out, pick the bumper up yourself. The dog gets nothing. Reset, try again. The moment the dog is quiet — even if it's just a second of quiet — mark it with "good dog" and send it. Then you build. A couple seconds of quiet today, five seconds tomorrow, ten next week. The dog learns that being quiet is what unlocks the retrieve it wants so badly. It's not doing it for you — it's doing it for itself, because quiet is the only behavior that gets rewarded.

One thing that often doesn't help: using the e-collar to correct the vocalization. In some cases it works fine, but in an anxious, high-drive dog, a correction can actually increase the anxiety and make things worse. The dog is already wound up — adding a stim on top of that can just push the energy higher. The reward-based approach tends to be more effective and more durable for this particular problem.

If the dog is so amped up that it can't hold it together for even a second, burn some energy first. Release the dog, throw a few bumpers with no pressure, let it shake some of it off. Then bring it back to heel, sit, mark, and run the drill. A dog that's had a minute of movement is usually easier to settle than one that's been holding tension in a blind for ten minutes before the first duck comes in. Use that in practice — don't just leave the dog locked in a sit for long periods while you work on steadiness. Mix in some run-and-retrieve, then come back to the controlled stuff.

One last thing worth mentioning: be careful how you respond when a dog is anxious, whether it's in the blind, at the vet, anywhere. Petting a whining dog, telling it "it's okay" in a soothing voice — that's rewarding the whining. I watched a woman at a vet's office stroking a barking spaniel the whole time, and the dog was barking at everyone who walked by. She thought she was calming it. She was training it. The same principle applies in the field: your response to the dog's behavior is always training something. Make sure you're training what you actually want.

Get after this early. The longer the pattern sets in, the harder it is to break. If your dog is just starting to develop vocalization, hit it now. Thanks for listening.
