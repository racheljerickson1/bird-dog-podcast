Transcript

Full transcript for Episode 45: Seasons End, Dog Training Timelines & Western Hunting Expo.

Tyce

Hey everyone, welcome to the Bird Dog Podcast. My name is Tyce. Today I want to do a quick season recap and then dig into a topic I get a lot of questions about: dog training timelines. What can you realistically expect from your dog and when?

Bird season is just wrapping up. We made it up to Idaho for the last week of the duck season — hunted the Snake River, had some fantastic dog work. The highlight was a water blind I hadn't planned to run. A drake goldeneye sailed on a hit, died and floated up against the far bank of the river, probably 250 yards away with the current. I had a boat. But I wanted to see if I could handle the dog there — across the main current, upstream, up onto the bank. Took a few casts, reading the current push, getting the angles right, and the dog made it, found the bird, and came back. That's the payoff for the year of training. You could have just as easily walked that retrieve in a boat, but having a dog that handles makes your hunting better in every scenario. Get your limit without running to the other side of the river. That's real-world value.

Also got out for a quick chucker afternoon. Hunted some private ground I hadn't been on before — classic chucker country, steep slopes, good views, good workout. Knocked down a couple wild birds over pointed dogs. One sailed on me and we couldn't locate it — always a bummer — but those are part of the game. Good day overall.

On to training timelines. These are generalities based on my program and experience — every dog is different, every trainer is different. But if you're training consistently, putting in four to five days a week, these ranges will be pretty accurate for most dogs.

It starts at the litter. If you're a breeder or you can ask your breeder to do this — starting around four weeks, I take a dead pheasant, duck, or chukar and just hold it near the puppies' noses in the whelping box. No agenda. Let them smell it. As they get a little older and start jumping around, the bird becomes something they chase, bat at, and grab. It builds prey drive and feather association before they've ever left their mom. If you're getting a puppy from a breeder, ask if they do any bird exposure. It makes a difference.

Standard pickup age is 7–8 weeks. As young as 6 weeks, as late as 12. Eight weeks is the sweet spot for most. When they arrive, your job is socialization, positive association with birds, basic exposure to water (if weather permits and water is 55°F or warmer), and general life experiences — new terrain, surfaces, sounds. Keep retrieves short: two to four bumpers, stop while the dog still wants more. You're building desire, not burning it out.

Between four and five months, the adult teeth are coming in. Gums can be sensitive. Dial back the retrieving slightly — especially with hard objects. High-drive dogs may not care at all, but if you notice a drop in retrieve desire or the dog getting mouthy, switch to softer objects and reduce pressure. Most adult teeth are fully in by five to six months.

Formal training starts at six months for most dogs. Soft or timid dogs, wait until seven to nine months — let them mature a little more. Bold, confident dogs can start earlier. At six months we begin what I call the foundation program: formal obedience, e-collar conditioning, force fetch, steadiness, water work, upland work, and shooting birds over the dog. A foundation dog at roughly ten months of age should be a solid junior hunter level — all obedience, force-fetched, steady, marking on land and water, working through decoys, delivering to hand. This is a genuinely useful hunting dog. Timeline: roughly four months of focused training.

From there, if you want to push toward senior hunter level work — handling, blind retrieves, double marks, memory birds — plan on another four months of training on top of that. Most dogs are running senior-level work around 14–16 months of age if you're training consistently. That's about eight months total of daily work starting from six months old.

Master hunter level is more dog-dependent. A truly exceptional dog with great genetics, handled well, and trained consistently can reach master level by around two years of age. That's a fast dog. Most dogs will get there between two and three years, and that's completely normal and nothing to stress over. Don't compare your dog's pace to someone else's. Work with what your dog gives you, build on it daily, and it'll get there.

The key is daily consistency. Short sessions more often beat long sessions once a week. Review what was learned, add something new, review again. That's how patterns get built into a dog's brain. Think of it like how you'd teach a kid math — you review, build, review, build — and eventually it all stacks up. A dog that trains four to five days a week, even if sessions are just fifteen minutes, will outperform a dog that trains one long session per week. Every time. Consistency is the program.

Thanks for listening. Hunt seasons are done but training season is just getting started. You've got five to six months before dove and grouse open again — use them. See you in the next one.
