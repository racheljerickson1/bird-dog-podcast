Transcript

Full transcript for Episode 42: Decoying Tips for Waterfowl Hunting.

Tyce

Hey everyone, welcome to the Bird Dog Podcast. My name is Tyce. Today I want to dig into waterfowl decoy setups — how I think about placement, motion, and what actually moves the needle on getting birds to commit.

The foundation of how I set up decoys is family groups. If you watch ducks or geese feeding in a field or resting on water, they're not spread out randomly — they're in loose clusters. A pair, maybe their young. Four to eight birds fairly close together, some space between groups. I replicate that. I'll build multiple family groups across the spread, leave open pockets between them for birds to land, and let the whole setup breathe. Compared to one big U-shape or blob, this gives landing birds multiple natural entry points, and if the wind shifts, you're not scrambling to redo everything. The landing zones are distributed, not just one fixed kill hole.

For motion: I run Mojo spinners, and my experience early on was that when those things first hit the market, birds had never seen them and they would absolutely hammer the spread. Three to one, we killed more birds with the spinners on. That's changed as hunting pressure has increased — birds that have been shot at over spinners will flare off them. If you're hunting pressured birds and they're not committing, pull the spinners and see what happens. Simple move, often makes a real difference.

For spinner placement: I don't run two side by side facing the same direction. I'll stagger them — one out front, one off the back left — so it looks like two birds landing at slightly different moments, not a matched pair dropping in unison. Always orient them into the wind. Ducks land into the wind, so your spinners should be facing into it. When the wind shifts, go out there and turn them. This is a detail a lot of people skip, and it costs them birds.

A newer addition to my spread is Mojo flicker tails — a small tail that spins and pulses within the family groups. I've used them the last couple seasons and they add subtle movement that catches the eye of birds looking down at the spread. It's not dramatic, but when birds are circling and deciding, that little flicker of motion in the family groups looks natural and pulls them in. Worth adding to your kit.

On calm water, my favorite motion tool is a pulsator — the Higdon Pulsated is what we've been running. It's a decoy that squirts water from underneath, creating a feeding-duck bob and ripple effect. On a dead-calm day it looks exactly like a bird tipping up to feed. The battery life has been solid, which matters since motion decoy batteries are always the weak point. What I like about the pulsator versus a swimmer is it stays put. Swimmer decoys, which jet back and forth on a cord, will often get the cord wrapped around adjacent decoys, especially in a tight spread or any current. The pulsator just bobs in place, creates good ripple, and stays where you put it.

The overall principle is this: if you can picture yourself as a bird looking down at your spread, what does it look like? Stale decoys with no movement on a calm day look fake — ducks always have ripple behind them, birds are always fidgeting and adjusting. Movement equals life. Spinners, flicker tails, pulsators, swimmers — the more natural movement you add, the more believable the spread looks from above. On overcast, calm days especially, that movement is doing a lot of the heavy lifting to convince birds to commit.

Keep your spread honest to what birds actually look like on the water. Family groups, natural spacing, movement that makes sense, and always wind-oriented. Get those basics right and the birds will do the rest. Thanks for listening.
