Transcript

Full transcript for Episode 23: (EP:23) Master AKC Hunt Test - Best of the best. Could your dog be a Master?.

Tyce

Hey everyone, welcome to the Bird Dog Podcast. My name is Tyce. I just finished a Master AKC hunt test — one of the most demanding evaluations in retriever work — and I want to walk you through what the master level looks like, how it's structured, and why it's worth pursuing if you're serious about your dog's development.

Quick structure breakdown: a Master test takes two days to complete one pass. Junior is one pass per day. Senior is one pass per day. Master requires two days — three series — to earn a single pass. To earn a Master Hunter title, you need five passes total, or six if you haven't earned a Senior Hunter title first. These passes bank indefinitely — you can accumulate them over multiple seasons — so there's no time pressure. Most dogs earn their Master title over one to three years.

Pass rates at a typical Master test with 60 dogs will usually be in the 30–50% range. Sometimes it's lower if the test is set up particularly hard. It just depends on conditions, test design, and how clean the dogs are running. At this test, close to 60 dogs ran and 29 passed — just under 50%. That's a solid pass rate for the level.

The marks at Master level are triples. Junior runs singles. Senior runs doubles. When you go to a triple, the complexity increases dramatically — you have three fall positions to remember, and they can be set at any angle. A bird can go left to right, right to left, back away from the line, across the other marks, into cover. The throwers can be positioned to wash the dog's memory — sending it for the easy mark first might mean it forgets one of the others. You have to know your dog, know which marks it's locked in on, and plan your pick-up order strategically. Standard best practice is outside-outside-center: pick up the farthest bird on each side first, then clean up the middle. That order tends to minimize interference between the falls.

This test's first series was a triple with a single blind. Live flyer to the right, a thrown bird to an alfalfa field at 130–150 yards, and another thrown to the edge of a sorghum field. After picking up all three, you ran a blind retrieve through a keyhole opening in goose decoys across a road. Then the dog honored at a nearby station — sitting steady while another dog worked. Honoring is evaluated at the Master level; your dog has to hold under full distraction while live birds are thrown and shot for another team. That's a genuine test of steadiness.

My dog went out of order on the first series — he wanted the center bird before the flyer and I let him go. When a dog is locked in on a mark and you fight him at the line, you often lose. His memory was on, he was confident, so I trusted him. That's part of handling at the Master level: knowing when to steer and knowing when to follow your dog's confidence. He cleaned up the series and we moved on. Two-day tests like this are a grind, but they're worth it. The Master title is a genuine achievement for dog and handler. If your dog is working at that level, get out and pursue it — it sharpens everything about your handling and your dog's training. Thanks for listening.
